How many Memory variants are there? More than anyone can finish.

There is no official catalogue of Memory variants — no registry, no standard, no publisher list. So this research set its own strict counting rule, sourced every named mode it could, and then counted what the rules themselves allow. The published floor is small. The space above it is not.

The short answer: Memory alone can support a large, growing catalogue without repeating the same game — your “unlimited puzzles” idea works for this family — provided a “new variant” means a new combination of rule dimensions, not a new picture set. The ceiling is not combinatorics; it is design quality. Most random combinations are trivial tweaks, unplayable, or confusing, so the catalogue has to grow along the rule dimensions, one deliberate step at a time.

The baseline

Classic Memory, in four moves

Every variant below is measured against this game: Concentration, also published as Memory, Pelmanism, Pexeso and Shinkei-suijaku — those are synonyms for the same game, not variants.

01 · LAYOUT

Cards are dealt face down in a layout. Nothing is visible.

02 · FLIP TWO

On a turn, a player flips two cards. Identical cards — or same rank and colour — are kept, and that player goes again.

03 · MISS

Non-matching cards turn back face down, in the same places. Remembering where they were is the whole game.

04 · WIN

Most pairs wins. Solo, the aim is to clear the board in the fewest non-matching turns.

Baseline source: Wikipedia — Concentration (card game)

The counting rule

What counts as a variant — and what doesn't

A mode is counted once, and only if it changes a rule: what counts as a match, what information you get, what a move does to the board, how turns work, or how you win. Anything less is the same game wearing a different coat. Any source claiming “exactly N variants” without stating its counting rule is marketing, not fact — so the rule is stated here instead.

COUNTS A real rule change

  • A different matchSame rank any colour, opposite colour only, three cards instead of two, or cards related by a rule rather than identical.
  • A different boardMisses move to new spots, cards swap mid-game, a card gets blocked by a token.
  • Different turnsNo extra turn for a match, one card at a time across two boards, or everyone flipping at once.
  • A different way to win or loseBeat the countdown, run out of moves or lives, solve the puzzle the matches reveal, finish a race.
  • Different informationStudy the board before it hides, different card backs that narrow the search, mismatches that stay face up.

DOESN'T COUNT Same game, new coat

  • Reskins and themesRavensburger sells Memory in Mario, Frozen, animal and vehicle editions — the rules text is identical in every listing.
  • Difficulty = pair count only8 vs 12 vs 16 pairs, 4×4 vs 4×8 grids. One guide states it plainly: “Difficulty only changes the number of cards. The rules, the flip-back pause and the scoring are the same at every size.”
  • Layout shape aloneWikipedia's Fancy (circle, triangle, diamond layouts) and Spaghetti (strewn cards) change where cards sit, not how a match is made.
  • A timer or score value alone60 s vs 120 s is tuning. Adding a countdown where none existed is a new rule — shortening one is not.
  • Synonyms, daily boards, photo decksNew names, a seeded daily board, achievements, or a “My Photos” deck change distribution or content, not play.

The anatomy

Nine rule dimensions do all the work

Grouped by what they change, the 30 variants concentrate in a few dimensions. These counts credit a variant to every dimension it touches (the filters above use primary dimension only), so they sum to more than 30. Match relation is the richest seam; match size is nearly untouched — no named published four-card variant was found anywhere.

One collapse worth knowing: word–picture, question–answer, equation–answer, capital–small letter, number–Roman numeral and “one more / one less” are one dimension with many contents — all replace “identical” with “related by a stated rule”. That single collapse is most of why the honest core count is 15–18, not 30.

The multiplication

From 30 named modes to six figures

The published catalogue is only the floor. Wikipedia itself notes the traditional variations “may be played in combination with one another”, and digital games already stack a timer on a move limit on helpers. The dimensions are independent — so they multiply.

Why doesn't this table multiply to 103,680? Because the ladder above deliberately uses conservative subsets of these options — 4 of the 8 helpers, 3 of the 4 visibility modes, 3 of the 4 layouts, and only six of the eleven dimensions at all. Multiply every count in this fuller table instead and the product is roughly 12–17 million, depending on whether match relation is counted at 6 or 8. Both figures are honest; the ladder is the floor, this table is the fuller space.

Quality control

Five tests separate a new game from a tweak

A combination earns its place in the catalogue if it changes at least one of these five things. If it changes none, it is tuning — sometimes tuning that matters, like a 0.8 s vs 5 s peek, but not a new game.

What counts as a matchRelation or size changes which cards you are hunting for.

What information is availableVisibility, card backs, previews, cards staying up — changes what your memory is of.

What a move does to the boardRelocation, shuffles, swaps, blocks, wilds and bombs decide whether remembered positions stay true.

How turns are allocatedExtra turn, One Flip, simultaneous, split-board — changes strategy and how much you leak to opponents. Under One Flip, revealing a known card to deny rivals information becomes the correct play.

How you win or loseMost pairs, beat the clock, survive on lives, solve the puzzle, win the race — changes what “good play” even means.

Degenerate combinations exist and must be filtered. A wild card plus opposite-colour-only matching can leave some cards unmatchable. Blind preview plus unmarked Chaos swaps is unfair. Simultaneous play plus a token-block can deadlock. A generator needs solvability and matchability checks, not just multiplication — the same lesson Mahjong Solitaire design stresses about winnable shuffles.

What this means for building Morphi

Two-thirds of the catalogue is data. One-third is engineering.

Data-only means the same flip-and-match view and code path, with different parameters in the level definition. New mechanics means new interaction, state, animation or opponent logic. This is exactly the split the refactored engine was built for: variants as manifest data first, features second.

DATA-ONLY Same view, new parameters

  • Pair count & grid — pairs, rows, cols
  • Theme & deck content — the card face asset list
  • Match relation — identical, rank-only, rank + colour, opposite colour, identical suit, via a matchKey function / pair-group id
  • Associated pairs — a pair-group id shared by two non-identical faces; optional two-array dealing
  • Trios — cardsPerTurn = 3 with groups of three, if the flip loop accepts N
  • Timed, move-limited, survival — timeLimit, moveLimit, lives plus lose conditions
  • Combo & penalty scoring — scoring formula constants and star thresholds
  • One Flip — one flag: extraTurnOnMatch
  • Hotseat 2–4 — a players[] array, a turn pointer, per-player scores
  • Daily / seeded board — a random seed
  • Flip-back delay — peekMs; difficulty tuning, not a new rule
  • Sound pairs — faces are audio clips instead of images; match logic unchanged

NEW MECHANICS New code, state or interaction

  • Moving misses, Chaos swaps, mid-game shuffle — positions must re-animate, the player's mental map is invalidated, swaps need a marker layer
  • Token-blocked / frozen cards — per-card blocked state, an expiry rule, a distinct visual state, flip validation
  • Wild, Take/Forfeit, Bomb cards — cards with actions instead of faces; Take/Forfeit mutate opponents' piles; Bomb can change solvability
  • Hint / Peek / Shuffle / Undo / +Time helpers — buttons, costs and cooldowns; Undo needs a full move history
  • Sequence playback inside Memory — a playback phase and order validation; Simon's mechanic grafted on, no longer pure flip-and-match
  • Simultaneous multiplayer — concurrency, card ownership and blocking; there is no turn loop at all
  • Versus AI — an opponent memory model, from fallible to perfect recall, plus turn timing
  • Split-board Duel — two independent boards, cross-board pair validation, alternating single flips
  • Rebus-reveal / tower-building wins — a second puzzle layer whose state advances on matches and decides the winner
  • Gravity / rotating board — a layout engine that collapses remaining cards, or positions remapped as the board turns
The practical implication: one engine can ship roughly two-thirds of the verified catalogue as level and mode parameters. The remaining third — movement, action cards, simultaneous play, AI, second-layer wins — needs one feature each, and stacking them all at once is where scope explodes. That argues for exactly your plan: grow the catalogue progressively, data-only variants first.

The boundary

Often confused with Memory — but different families

The test: does the core loop stay “flip hidden cards from a fixed layout, remember positions, remove matched sets”? If a different loop replaces it, it is a different family — however much it also “matches” or “tests memory”. Rule of thumb: if removing the memory element still leaves a playable game, it was never a Memory variant.

Simon

Looks like Memory becauseIt is called a memory game and tests recall.

Why it isn'tNo hidden board and no pairs: the device shows a growing light-and-sound sequence and you repeat it in order. Sequence recall vs spatial pair recall.

Mahjong Solitaire

Looks like Memory becauseMatch identical pairs to clear a board.

Why it isn'tTiles are face up throughout; only “free” tiles — open side, nothing on top — can be taken. The skill is spatial unblocking in a 3D stack, not remembering hidden positions.

Match-3 (Bejeweled, Candy Crush)

Looks like Memory becauseMatch identical items to clear them.

Why it isn'tYou swap adjacent tiles to create lines of 3+. Matches don't pre-exist as hidden pairs, and nothing is remembered face-down.

Onet / Connect-2

Looks like Memory becauseFind identical pairs to clear a board, timed, with hint and shuffle boosters.

Why it isn'tTiles are face up, and a pair is legal only if a path of at most 2 turns connects them unblocked. Path planning replaces position memory.

Go Fish

Looks like Memory becauseCollect matching sets, partly by remembering who holds what.

Why it isn'tCards live in hands and are won by asking opponents. Bluffing and asking replace flipping a shared face-down layout.

Kim's Game

Looks like Memory becauseA classic visual-memory test, sometimes sold as a memory game.

Why it isn'tOne study phase, then recall from memory — list the objects, or spot the removed one. No flip-pair loop, no layout to clear.

Spot It! / Dobble

Looks like Memory becauseFast matching of symbols.

Why it isn'tEvery card is face up; the race is to spot the one shared symbol between two cards. No hidden information at all — perceptual speed, not memory.

Trio / Nana

Looks like Memory becauseCollect trios of the same rank; a team version exists.

Why it isn'tCards are dealt to hands plus a face-down centre, and trios are found by revealing from hands — not by flipping a shared grid. Its “set of 3” doesn't make Memory-trios the same game.

Concentration (TV show)

Looks like Memory becauseIt is directly based on Memory / Pelmanism.

Why it isn't (quite)A hybrid: pair matching is retained, but matches reveal a rebus and the rebus solve decides the winner. Its special cards — Wild, Take, Forfeit — are counted as variants above; the show as a whole is Memory plus puzzle-solving.

Domino Concentration

Looks like Memory becauseIt is explicitly “an adaptation of Concentration”.

Why it's borderlineIt keeps the flip-pair loop but replaces fixed pairs with a sum-to-12 relation, so it is counted in the catalogue with a caveat. Under a strict fixed-pairs definition it moves to this table and the headline count drops by one.

Honest gaps

What the research could not verify

  • No published four-card (quad), rotating-board, or gravity/collapse Memory variant exists in the sources found. They appear only as plausible procedural options in the combinatorics — not in the verified catalogue.
  • “Randomly moving cards” in computer Memory rests on a single Wikipedia sentence with no title, version or rule detail; whether cards drift continuously or swap discretely could not be verified.
  • Bomb and Skip helpers are documented for Memory mainly in app-store descriptions and one code tutorial, not a formal rulebook; their exact costs and effects vary by app.
  • No single source enumerates all Memory variants. The counts on this page are the research's stated counting convention applied to the sources found — not a quoted industry total.
  • Team Memory is attested in educational use (teams taking turns), but no distinct published team rule beyond shared turn-taking was found.
