Mahjong Solitaire, how it is played

One player, one stacked board of mahjong tiles, one simple action: pick two free tiles that match and watch the pile open up. Clear all 144 tiles to win — run out of free pairs and the board wins.

This is the solo puzzle only. Although it borrows the tiles and the name, “the method of gameplay is unrelated” to 4-player Mahjong — no hands, no turns, no opponents. (Wikipedia)

01 · The game in one page

The core loop

The whole layout is dealt face-up at once — there is no hand, no draw pile, no opponent turn and no discarding. The only action in the game is choosing a pair, so all of the strategy lives in which pair you take first: every removal changes what becomes free next.

1

A full board is dealt

144 tiles are stacked in layers in a named layout — classically the Turtle — all visible at once, most of them covered or blocked.

2

Pick two free tiles that match

Click or tap one free tile, then its match. If both are free and their faces pair up, they are playable.

3

The pair disappears

Tiles directly underneath may be uncovered, and tiles beside the pair may gain an open side. Removing tiles can never trap a tile that was already free.

4

Repeat to empty — or stuck

Keep peeling the mound down until the board is empty, or until no free matching pair remains.

✓ Win — board empty

All pairs removed. In the standard game that is 72 pairs: “You win the moment the last two tiles disappear.” (learn-mahjong.com)

✕ Loss — no free pair left

Tiles remain, but no exposed, free matching pair exists — a deadlock. The game “is won when all pairs of tiles have been removed from the board, and lost if the remaining tiles contain no exposed pairs.” (Wikipedia) In timed modes, the clock running out is an extra way to lose.

A deadlock is not the same as an unwinnable deal: a perfectly solvable board can be played into a dead end by pairing the wrong two of four identical copies — and a randomly dealt board can be unwinnable from the start. See Solvability below.

On a real board the same test applies: a tile you could not slide out sideways or lift straight up is not free. 03 · The one rule that matters most

What makes a tile “free”

A visible face is not enough. A tile is free — open, exposed, playable — only when both conditions hold at the same time.

1 · Nothing on top

The tile must be topmost for its position. If another tile rests even partly on top of it, it is buried. Overlap counts from any higher layer, not just a tile centred directly above: in the Turtle, the single peak tile sits offset over the 2×2 layer beneath and covers all four tiles at once — none of them is free until it goes. (learn-mahjong.com; everygamemade.com)

五六

The peak tile overlaps both tiles below — both stay buried while it sits there.

2 · One side open

At least one long side — left or right — must have no tile directly beside it at the same level. A tile boxed in on both sides is locked even with a clear top. Tiles above or below it in the same layer do not count for this rule — only direct left/right neighbours. That is why row ends are always the first place to look, and middles of long rows stay locked until an end is peeled away. (learn-mahjong.com)

End tiles can slide out — free. Middle tiles are boxed in on both sides — locked.

Near-universal table manners (UI conventions, not rules): free tiles are drawn brighter, blocked tiles dimmed or greyed, and the selected tile is highlighted. Many games add an “always highlight free tiles” or “highlight matches for the selected tile” option for beginners.

04 · The tiles

The standard 144-tile set

Matching is by exact face — same suit and number, same wind, or same dragon colour. A Bamboo 3 never matches a Character 3 or a Dot 3. Most faces exist in four identical copies, so you often choose which two of four to pair — and choosing the wrong two is a classic way to strand the others. (freesolitaire.com)

Pairs in total

108 + 28 + 8 = 144 tiles = 72 pairs in the classic game. Smaller layouts use subsets — boards of 72, 74, 94 or 108 tiles are all common.

The one exception: Flowers & Seasons

Any Flower matches any other Flower. Any Season matches any other Season. The four pictures in each group differ, but they pair as a group. A Flower never matches a Season, and neither matches a suit or honour tile.

Why: each bonus picture occurs only once, so exact matching would make these tiles impossible to remove. Group matching is what keeps them clearable. (arcadiagames.io)

The two groups

Flowers — the “Four Gentlemen”: Plum, Orchid, Chrysanthemum, Bamboo. (learn-mahjong.com/tiles)

Seasons: Spring, Summer, Autumn, Winter.

The same group rule is usually kept in Triple variants — any 3 Flowers, any 3 Seasons.

05 · Boards & stacking

Layouts: same rules, different mountains

Tiles sit in layers on a grid, upper layers offset so they straddle the tiles below. Overlap is the difficulty engine: a lower tile can stay hidden for most of the game, and the board is always dismantled top-down. Only shape, tile count, depth and the number of free tiles change between layouts — modern catalogues run from 24–50+ boards to 650+ on one site.

What actually makes a layout hard

Not tile count alone. Six drivers, in the research:

Depth

Taller stacks hide more tiles for longer and reduce what you know early.

Opening freedom

Free pairs at the start: Fortress often offers only 5–6 live pairs, Spider about 16. Fewer opening moves = less room for error.

Row length

Long rows lock their middles; narrow, tall layouts restrict horizontal peeling.

Choke points

Shapes that seal areas off — the Turtle’s head and tail, Fortress’s corner towers.

Hidden proportion

Only 35 of 144 Turtle tiles are free at the start, so early play is partly blind. Layouts that expose more faces play easier at the same tile count.

Endgame tightness

The last 20–30 tiles are often the hardest: one wrong pairing of a quad can strand a tile with no partner.

06 · Variations & assists

How implementations soften, score and stretch the game

The matching and free-tile rules are stable worldwide. Almost everything around them — clocks, hints, shuffles, scoring — varies by implementation. Treat every number in this section as one game’s choice, not “the” rule.

Timed vs untimed

  • Untimed / relaxed is the classic default: no clock, no penalty for thinking. Many modern apps advertise “no timer” as the calm mode.
  • Timed: clear the board before a limit — sprint modes include 2-minute sprints and 15-minute score attacks; remaining seconds often convert to bonus points.
  • Time as score, not a limit: even untimed games commonly record completion time for daily rankings and personal bests.

Hints

  • Highlights a legal free pair — but a hint is often random, not the pair that frees the most tiles. Some newer games claim smarter hints; don’t assume it.
  • Usually limited (e.g. 3–5 per game) or unlimited in Zen / relaxed modes; sometimes costs points.

Shuffle / reshuffle

  • Re-deals the faces of the tiles still on the board, keeping the same layout positions and layers. The shape never changes.
  • Classically offered when no free pair remains; many games also allow a voluntary shuffle at any time.
  • Limits vary widely: 3 per game (NitroPlayer), 5 per game (several classic implementations, incl. the Mahjong Titans tradition), unlimited, or exactly 1 free reshuffle on a dead end. Some charge points (e.g. 50) or pay a bonus for winning without shuffling (e.g. +200).
  • Some games auto-reshuffle, free, when no moves remain; others prompt first.

Undo

  • Reverses the last pair — sometimes several moves, sometimes unlimited.
  • Used to test a match, back out of a dead end, or fix a misclick.
  • Often scored: e.g. +100 bonus for winning without undo in one implementation; free in others. Unlimited undo makes a solvable board effectively always recoverable if you backtrack far enough.

Scoring & combos — no standard exists

  • Examples found, all site-specific: 10 points per pair (NitroPlayer); 100 per pair elsewhere; 95 per triple-group in Triple Mahjong.
  • Height bonus: pairs from higher layers score more.
  • Speed & streaks: fast consecutive matches build a “Combo ×N” multiplier that resets when play stalls. (mahjong-solitaire.me)
  • Clean-play bonuses: no-undo (+100) and no-shuffle (+200) on TrySolitaire; hint / shuffle penalties elsewhere.

Because scoring is not standardised, a new game can define its own — but it must teach it, since players can’t transfer expectations from another version.

Daily vs endless

  • Daily challenge: one new board per day, the same deal for every player, ranked by completion time, with streaks for consecutive days; fixed (often Turtle) or rotating layout. (nitroplayer.com; trysolitaire.com)
  • Endless / free play: unlimited random deals of a chosen layout, no shared leaderboard, no streak.
  • Level progression (mobile apps): sequential stages with escalating layouts, achievements and unlockables — a different retention loop from daily puzzles.

When you hit a dead end — what games actually do

Solvability — the design-critical nuance

A randomly dealt board is not guaranteed winnable: Wikipedia cites an analysis of ten million Turtle games finding ~3% unsolvable even with perfect information (looking under tiles allowed). Many modern implementations therefore deal backwards from a solved state — repeatedly remove free pairs from a full board and assign faces in reverse, guaranteeing a winning sequence exists. (Wikipedia; dev48v.medium.com)

And a guaranteed-solvable deal can still be played into a dead end by pairing the wrong two of a quad — which is exactly why undo and shuffle exist. Solvability of the deal ≠ solvability of your path through it.

07 · Not to be confused with

Solitaire vs its three look-alikes

All four use mahjong tiles. Almost nothing else transfers — which is why search results confuse them, and why the solo game should be named “Mahjong Solitaire”, not just “Mahjong”.

vs Mahjong Connect / Shisen-Sho — path matching

  • Board: flat, single-layer rectangular grid; every tile visible at once. Solitaire is multi-layer and stacked, with most tiles starting hidden or blocked.
  • Rule: the free-tile rule is replaced by a path rule — two identical tiles clear if a line with no more than two 90° turns (three straight segments) joins them without crossing other tiles; the path may run around the outside of the board. (onlinemahjong.com)
  • Strategy: border-first scanning and keeping middle paths open, since border pairs almost always have an outside route.
  • Dead ends differ: in Connect a pair can exist yet be unconnectable; in Solitaire a pair can exist yet be unfree.

vs Tile Triple / Triple Mahjong — tray matching

  • Match size: three identical tiles per clear, not two. In classic Triple Mahjong, three free tiles are selected directly from the board. (plays.org)
  • Tray variant (most mobile “Tile Triple” games): tapping sends a tile to a holding tray, usually 7 slots. Three identical tiles in the tray vanish; if the tray fills with no triple, the level is lost — a completely different loss condition. (Google Play listing)
  • Kept from Solitaire: the free-tile rule, and usually the Flower / Season group rule (any 3 Flowers, any 3 Seasons).
  • Contrast: Solitaire removal is immediate and reversible; Triple-tray removal is deferred, creating memory and tray-management pressure Solitaire doesn’t have.
08 · Screens & input

Phones vs desktop — and why wide screens suit this game

The interaction model is the same everywhere: select one tile, select its match, with a highlight on the pick and brighter free tiles. What changes is how much board you can legibly fit.

On desktop

  • Mouse click to select, click the second tile to match.
  • Scroll or on-screen buttons to navigate large layouts; keyboard navigation (tab / arrows) in some open-source versions.
  • A mouse can hit small, densely overlapped tiles — precision is not the bottleneck.

On phones & tablets

  • Tap to select, tap the match; pinch-zoom and drag to pan; rotate to landscape to enlarge tiles.
  • The core problem: a full 144-tile Turtle shrunk into portrait makes tiles “the size of a fingernail” — Mahjong Bay’s own description, which motivated its narrow, tall Three Sails layout for upright phones. (mahjongbay.com)
  • Common fixes: pinch / zoom + pan, a zoom bar for devices without multi-touch, auto-zoom that expands the board as outer tiles clear, and optional board rotation in portrait. (ffalt.itch.io)
  • Some browser games instead size tiles to the screen so the whole layout fits with no zooming, accepting smaller tiles and leaning on bright free-tile highlighting for aim. (nitroplayer.com)
  • Guide tip, verbatim in spirit: if you keep mis-tapping, rotate to landscape — the tiles get bigger.

Wide is native here

The Turtle is wider than it is tall. A wide screen shows the full board at large, legible size with no zoom required — an advantage, not a stretch goal.

Portrait is the hard problem

Observed options: shrink-to-fit (legibility loss), pinch / pan / rotate (interaction cost), separate portrait layouts — e.g. “Portrait Lite” for phones, “Portrait Full” for tablets, “Landscape Full” for desktops (zenoworks.studio) — or purpose-built narrow layouts like Three Sails (94 tiles).

Use the side space

On wide screens keep the board centred and put score, remaining pairs, hint / undo / shuffle and the layout picker in side panels — not overlaid on tiles. Auto-zoom remains useful late-game as the cluster shrinks.

09 · Origin

A computer game, not an ancient one

The tiles are old; the solitaire game is not. “Unlike patience card games, Mahjong Solitaire has no well-documented history before the computer age” — the “ancient Chinese Turtle game” story is described by Wikipedia as Activision marketing hype that Lockard himself never claimed.

1800s, second half — China

4-player mahjong takes shape in the Qing dynasty, growing out of older Chinese card games. This is the origin of the tiles, not of the solitaire puzzle. (learn-mahjong.com/history)

1981 — Brodie Lockard, PLATO

Lockard writes Mah-Jongg on the PLATO system, played on a CDC-721 touch-screen terminal and released free. It stacked traditional tiles into a mound and removed matching exposed pairs until clear. The starting mound was the Turtle.

1986 — Activision’s Shanghai

The commercial breakthrough: IBM PC, Macintosh, Amiga, Atari ST and Apple IIgs, with the Mac version by Lockard; around 10 million copies sold. “Shanghai” was Activision’s trademark — which is why clones used other names.

1990s — Taipei & Mahjong Titans

Taipei appears in Microsoft Entertainment Packs for Windows 3.x (1990). Premium Windows Vista / 7 later ship Mahjong Titans with six layouts — Turtle, Dragon, Fortress, Crab, Spider and more.

Since then

Countless web and mobile versions. Other names for the same game: Shanghai Solitaire, Taipei, Kyodai, The Turtle, Mahjongg. (Wikipedia)

10 · Where beginners go wrong

Common mistakes & rules players get wrong

1 Trying to match blocked tiles

Clicking a covered or both-sides-boxed tile and expecting it to work — or assuming a visible face means playable. Both free conditions must hold.

2 Hunting for an identical Flower / Season

Each bonus picture occurs once, so the identical partner doesn’t exist. Any Flower with any Flower; any Season with any Season; never across groups.

3 Confusing look-alikes

1 of Bamboo (a bird — sparrow / peacock) is a suit tile needing an identical bird, not a Flower; the leafy Bamboo Flower is a different tile again. High Circles vs high Bamboo (7/8/9) differ by a single dot or stick — count, don’t judge the shape. Characters 4 / 6 / 8 differ in small strokes. The White Dragon is often a near-blank, blue-framed tile that blends into the board — it still needs a partner. (learn-mahjong.com/tiles)

4 Grabbing the first available pair

The biggest strategic mistake: clearing an easy edge pair that frees nothing, instead of a pair that opens a tall stack or a long row. Play top-down, then outside-in, and prefer pairs that reveal new faces.

5 Pairing the wrong two of a quad

With three or four copies free, taking the first two can strand the rest under tiles that later become unfreeable. Compare which copies block more underneath before pairing. (freesolitaire.com)

6 Clearing one side only

Sweeping one side clean while the other stays stacked can trap partners on the cleared side with no free counterpart.

7 Ignoring tall stacks

Leaving 4–5-high peaks untouched while clearing edges seals the buried centre tiles — the Turtle in particular “punishes you for ignoring the centre.”

8 Assuming every deal is winnable

In random-deal games some boards are unwinnable; in solvable-deal games a dead end usually means a wrong pairing several moves earlier — undo, not a new deal, is the fix.

9 Expecting the wrong game

Expecting draw / discard and melds (4-player), a path rule (Connect), or any identical pair anywhere to be playable regardless of blocking.

11 · For the wide-screen build

Design notes for a full-screen version

Synthesis from the research, for planning a wide-screen Mahjong Solitaire as the next Morphi family — a centred, full-board game that uses the wide template the way the Turtle was always meant to be seen.

Default to the full Turtle

It is the genre’s recognisable default and natively wide. Offer Fortress, Dragon, Crab, Spider and Bridge as real difficulty / shape variants — their opening free-pair counts differ materially, so they are not reskins.

Guarantee solvability

Deal backwards from a solved state, or label a random-deal mode explicitly as “may be unwinnable”. Players can’t tell an unwinnable deal from their own mistake — that ambiguity is the genre’s classic frustration.

Make free vs blocked unmistakable

Bright vs dimmed, plus optional “always highlight free tiles” and “highlight matches for the selected tile”. The single highest-value accessibility feature, especially with look-alike faces.

Teach the two exceptions early

A first-time tutorial should explicitly show Flower ↔ Flower and Season ↔ Season — and that the bird is 1 of Bamboo — before the first board.

Dead-end UX, in order

Detect “no free pair” immediately and offer: (1) undo to the last branch, (2) shuffle the remaining tiles, stating how many shuffles are left, (3) restart the same deal. Don’t silently declare a loss while a rescue exists, and don’t silently auto-shuffle if the player values a clean score.

Scoring is yours — so teach it

There is no standard to inherit. If the game uses combos, height bonuses or no-undo / no-shuffle bonuses, surface them in-game rather than relying on transfer from other versions.

Treat portrait as its own problem

Ship portrait-specific narrow / tall, fewer-tile layouts, or a pinch / pan / rotate mode. Do not squash the Turtle. On wide screens, side space goes to score, remaining pairs, assists and the layout picker.

Daily and endless can coexist

Daily = same deal for everyone, time-ranked, with streaks. Endless = unlimited random deals or level progression. They serve different retention loops.

Open questions the research could not settle

  • Turtle layer count: sources say 4 or 5 layers — a counting convention, not a different board. The tile counts (87 / 36 / 16 / 4 / 1) are consistent.
  • Fortress size: 144 tiles in one implementation, 114 in another. Layout definitions are not standardised — verify any specific layout’s positions against the version being built or licensed, not against the generic name.
  • Scoring and assist limits are implementation-specific throughout; no single authoritative table exists.
  • No reliable pre-computer history for the solitaire game was found — the “ancient Turtle game” origin should not be presented as fact.
